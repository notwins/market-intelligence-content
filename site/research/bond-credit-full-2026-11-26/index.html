1. What changed: three takeaways

Treasuries rallied even as the growth and inflation releases remained strong. The 25 November official 10Y and 30Y par yields fell 6bp each to 4.85% and 5.24%. BEA revised Q3 real GDP growth to 4.3% annualised and reported October core PCE inflation of 3.3% year-on-year. A flight to safety need not mean the inflation problem has disappeared. [S1, S5, S6, R1]

AI credit is becoming more differentiated. S&P placed Nvidia's AA ratings on negative CreditWatch on 25 November, not an actual downgrade. Reuters reported distressed pricing for CoreWeave and sharply higher SoftBank protection costs. A weaker equity quote, CDS price or rating watch does not establish missed payments. [S8, R2]

New Fed research challenges a simple margin-call narrative. In its measured sponsored Treasury repo segment, hedge-fund transaction haircuts did not materially increase during March 2020 stress, although other margin channels did. This is not a finding that retail wealth-financing limits never change. [S7]

2. Four core research and data deep dives

2.1 Federal Reserve: The Surprising Stability of Hedge Fund Repo Haircuts

25 November 2026; Samuel J. Hempel, R. Jay Kahn, Mia R. Kurta and Ioana Neamtu; FEDS Notes, full HTML reviewed, sections 2–5, Table 1 and Figures 2–4. Original research.

Contractual haircuts were fairly stable during the studied market shocks. The authors use OFR centrally cleared repo trades from November 2019 to September 2026, matching transactions for the same Treasury CUSIP within 15 minutes. Hedge-fund borrowing haircuts were near zero after 2021; money-market fund lending haircuts clustered around 2%. Futures margins rose markedly in March 2020 despite the stable measured repo haircuts.

Methodological boundary: only 45% of the relevant sponsored MMF/HF volume matched within the narrow window. Positioning: test cash availability, leverage, settlement timing, funding renewal and the actual lender contract independently. A stable repo haircut does not prevent losses when the collateral price falls. Client explanation: even when the lender does not change its borrowing rules, falling collateral prices can leave you with less room to borrow.

7. Original duration, spread and financing scenarios

Explicitly hypothetical, not bank policy or market data. Assume $1m bond assets, modified duration 16, convexity 300, $700k debt, 75% permitted LTV, no fees/coupons/defaults. Delta yield in decimal. Price return ≈ −16 × Δy + 150 × Δy². Assets = starting assets × (1 + return); translated debt = initial debt × (1 + liability FX change); equity = assets − translated debt; headroom = LTV × assets − debt. External cash needed = max(0, −headroom). Sale of collateral with proceeds reducing loan = shortfall / (1 − LTV), only if feasible.

Even unchanged haircuts do not eliminate margin risk. At +100bp, the $145,000 market loss equals 48⅓% of starting equity; external cash repayment $58,750 or collateral sale $235,000 would restore the modelled limit, excluding costs. If LTV falls to 70%, headroom is −$101,500; if liability currency then appreciates 5%, translated debt becomes $735,000 and headroom −$136,500. The required collateral sale then becomes $455,000. No double subtraction of financing from net NAV.

Separate corporate spread case: assume duration 7 and convexity 60 for a synthetic corporate bond, Treasury +50bp and spread +100bp. With equal rate and spread sensitivity assumed, −7×0.015 +30×0.015² = −0.09825 (−9.825%). A $1m standalone position loses $98,250. Default and liquidity jumps are excluded. For actual corporate bonds rate and spread durations can differ. Extra 100bp floating funding cost on unchanged $700,000 adds $7,000 annualised; with $735,000 translated debt, $7,350, ignoring accrual timing, hedges and fees.
