1. What changed

AI borrowing has become a broader financial-stability issue. The Bank of England (BoE) expects hyperscaler bond issuance to exceed US$305bn in 2026, approximately 8% of worldwide IG issuance. This is a full-year estimate, not completed issuance as of today. In its section 1.4 and footnote 4, the Bank estimates potential hyperscaler exposure above US$4tn, including outstanding debt, leases, purchasing obligations and contingent guarantees. Do not add these to new bond issuance. [S1]

Higher funding costs are reaching weaker borrowers. The same report cites Fitch's US private-credit default rate at 6.3% for the trailing year to September, versus 6.0% to June and 5.4% a year earlier. S&P's public actions list shows Jaguar Land Rover downgraded to BB+ on fiscal-2027 cash burn, and Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles/TML Holdings to BBB− on JLR's weak performance. These are rating changes, not bankruptcies. [S1, S6]

Policy is not uniformly moving toward easier money. The ECB's October meeting account indicates substantial support for a rate increase even though the meeting ended without one. The BoE's capital review is a distinct prudential reform, not a policy-rate reduction. Singapore's new retail-fund authorisation process is a separate regulatory development. [S2–S4]

The new risk is the overlap of creditors and repayment sources. AI customers can also be investors or suppliers to the same projects; lease and guarantee structures may make headline debt an incomplete indicator. Lower-grade borrowers have suffered a disproportionate repricing. The BoE nevertheless reports orderly gilt markets and limited cross-market spillovers so far. Chart 1.6 uses sterling money market data to 12 November; chart 1.9 uses UK securities-financing data as of 30 October. Short repo maturities can force deleveraging even if underlying sovereign obligations remain sound.

Mortgage refinancing is a forward risk, not evidence of default today. Chart 2.1 uses the OIS curve on 18 November and H1 2026 mortgage stocks (BoE, Bloomberg, FCA product sales, Bank calculations). Around 7m of approximately 9m owner-occupier mortgages are projected to refinance by December 2029. The model excludes buy-to-let, some off-balance-sheet loans and mortgages below £1,000. The higher-payment forecast depends on market-implied refinancing rates. Current mortgage and consumer arrears remain close to historical averages. Private-credit measures have valuation lags and incomplete coverage; the underlying Fitch sample was not retrieved. Chart 1.11 observations are through 18 November except leveraged loans (13 November); no chart is copied.

Bond implication: separate sovereign duration from credit spreads, and keep liquidity against simultaneous shocks. Client explanation: even a sound bond can be sold at a loss if the person financing it suddenly needs cash.

Capital requirements may become more proportionate, but individual creditor protection still differs. The overall review's prospective normal-time reduction is equivalent to roughly 50bp of RWA, inclusive of separate leverage reforms; this must not be added to the 30bp risk-weighted change as if independent. The FPC benchmark remains around 13%, and the UK neutral countercyclical buffer is 2%. The PRA will consult on changes: proposed requirements are not yet uniformly implemented. Bond implication: compare senior, Tier 2 and AT1 instruments individually; the regulatory reform is not a guarantee of repayment. Client explanation: a bank can lend more efficiently without every type of bond becoming safer.

Published 26 November; official meeting record. Original account (French locale, English text). English-locale retrieval failed; the official French-locale document displayed the English account. Sections on financial-market developments and policy discussion reviewed.

Faster approval is not unrestricted retail access to private credit. A new non-traditional funds appendix takes effect immediately; applicable law, custody and liquidity protections remain. Explicit synthetic assumptions only: USD 1,000,000 asset market value; modified duration 16, convexity 300, loan USD 700,000, maximum LTV 75%. Equal duration for combined rate/spread shock in the interactive illustration. Price change ≈ −16 × dy + 150 × dy², dy = (rate + spread bps)/10,000. Assets = initial assets × (1 + change); translated loan = initial loan × (1 + FX change/100); net equity = assets − translated loan; headroom = LTV × assets − loan. External repayment = max(0, −headroom); collateral sale = repayment/(1−LTV) if all proceeds reduce debt. Annual incremental interest = translated loan × funding shock /10,000. DV01 initially USD 1,600 per bp (linear approximation). Excludes coupon, tax, default, fees and security-level recognition rules.

Margin shortfall is not the market loss. +100bp implies $145,000 asset loss; $58,750 external cash repayment restores 75% LTV. Liquidating existing collateral requires $235,000 because the collateral base shrinks too. At 70% LTV the headroom becomes −$101,500; a further 5% adverse liability FX translation produces −$136,500 headroom, requiring $455,000 of sale if all proceeds repay debt. +100bp funding on unchanged $700,000 principal adds $7,000 annual interest. A separate corporate portfolio with modified duration 7, convexity 60 and combined +150bp shock (rates +50bp and spread +100bp) loses approximately 9.825%, or $98,250 per $1m. Nonparallel shifts, default, security optionality and FX on assets are outside the model. Do not subtract financing twice where NAV is already net.
